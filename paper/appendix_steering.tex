\section{Steering arms, balance and power}
\label{app:steering}

\paragraph{The manipulation check in full.} Steering \emph{desperate} moves the
desperate projection, measured on a clean re-encoding after the steering hooks are
removed, from $-0.656$ at $-0.5$ through $-0.636$ at $-0.3$ and $-0.616$ unsteered
to $-0.580$ at $+0.3$ and $-0.569$ at $+0.5$. The unsteered standard deviation is
$0.075$, so the $+0.3$ shift is $0.48$ SD. Mann--Whitney against unsteered gives
$p = 0.019$, $0.30$, $0.025$ and $0.012$ at $-0.5$, $-0.3$, $+0.3$ and $+0.5$. For
scale, hacking and non-hacking trials differ by $1.12$ SD on the same readout, and
that contrast is itself length-entangled (\S\ref{sec:len}).

\paragraph{The coarse dose--response grid.} Table~\ref{tab:steer} shows the
$\pm0.3$ arms, which carry the directional claim. The trend test also uses a
fine-grained sweep at $\pm0.05$, $\pm0.10$ and $\pm0.15$ (480 trials) not tabulated
here. Reward-hacking counts on the coarse grid are, for \emph{desperate}, $3/40$, $4/40$, $4/40$, $2/39$, $1/39$ and $1/37$ at
$-0.5$, $-0.3$, $-0.2$, $+0.2$, $+0.3$ and $+0.5$; and for \emph{calm}, $1/40$,
$4/40$, $2/40$, $7/40$, $3/39$ and $5/40$. Neither arm is monotone in strength.

\paragraph{The arms are not balanced across task variants.} Base rates differ
sharply across the four \texttt{fast\_sum} variants, at $4/30$, $0/30$, $9/30$ and
$1/30$ unsteered. The \emph{desperate} arm at $+0.3$ covers all four. The random arm at $+0.3$ covers only
variants 1 and 2, whose combined unsteered rate is $4/60$ against $14/120$ overall. The pooled contrast therefore confounds the direction
being steered with the mix of tasks it was steered on, and it does so in
\emph{desperate}'s favour: \emph{desperate} was steered on the higher-rate tasks, so
an inert intervention would still have produced a larger number there. It did not.

Restricting both arms to the two shared variants gives $0/20$ for \emph{desperate}
at $+0.3$ against $9/100$ for random directions and $4/60$ unsteered. The direction
of the result is unchanged, but with zero events in 20 trials the comparison is
almost uninformative (Fisher $p = 0.35$). We report both, and we do not claim the
matched contrast as evidence for anything beyond consistency with the unmatched one.

\paragraph{How the random directions were built.} Each of the five is drawn iid
Gaussian in the residual basis, Gram--Schmidt projected orthogonal to all 50
emotion vectors, then rescaled to the mean norm of those vectors. They are
therefore matched on norm and on orthogonality to the emotion subspace. They are
\emph{not} matched on natural activation variance or on logit-lens impact, so they
bound the effect of an arbitrary equally-large perturbation outside the emotion
subspace, which is a weaker claim than matching on downstream influence would give.

\paragraph{The random arm is five directions, not 100 draws.} Rollouts are nested
within five sampled directions, so treating the 100 trials as independent draws
overstates the precision of the random-direction null. The per-direction rates at
$+0.3$ are $2/20$, $3/20$, $2/20$, $0/20$ and $2/20$, which is homogeneous enough
that pooling does not hide a direction behaving like \emph{desperate}. The
specificity claim should still be read as applying to these five matched directions
rather than to arbitrary directions in general.

\paragraph{The base rate caps what could have been detected.} Our unsteered rate is
$11.7\%$, so no multiplicative increase above about $8.5\times$ is attainable in this
design at all. \citet{sofroniew2026} report ${\sim}14\times$. The elicitation setting
therefore cannot test an effect of the reported magnitude, only much smaller ones,
and this is a property of the design rather than a result.

\paragraph{Steering did not change how much text was produced.} Response length is
the nuisance variable of \S\ref{sec:len}, so a steering null would be hard to read
if the intervention had also shortened the outputs. It did not: mean response length
is $1402$ characters unsteered, $1324$ in the emotion arm at $|s| \ge 0.3$ and $1456$
in the random arm. The behavioral null is not an artifact of shorter generations.

\paragraph{The $V_{\text{text}}$ pool is mostly steered trials.} The $n = 992$ set in
\S\ref{sec:vtext} is $952$ steered and $40$ unsteered, and $V_{\text{int}}$ is the
coordinate the steering manipulates, so that comparison could in principle be
flattered by the manipulation. Splitting the pool shows it is not.
$V_{\text{int}}$ reaches AUC $0.832$ on steered trials against $0.900$ on unsteered,
and $V_{\text{text}}$ reaches $0.646$ against $0.660$. The ordering holds in both
and the gap is large in both, though not equal: $0.186$ on steered trials against
$0.240$ on unsteered, the latter on 40 trials with 7 events. The tie-structure
argument does not turn on the pool's composition, but that slice is too small to
compare the gaps.

\paragraph{How many labels would have to be wrong.} Neither judge is checked
against humans (\S3) and there are 14 events, so the labels are the least
validated input to \S\ref{sec:dirs}. Taking the strongest surviving direction,
\texttt{bored}, and flipping judge labels adversarially: its statistic falls from
$T = 38.7$ to $24.5$ under the single worst flip and to $16.2$ under the worst two.
Family-wise significance under the conditional null sits near $T$ 12 to 14, so two wrong labels would leave the direction significant but close to the
boundary, and a handful more would not. This sensitivity analysis does not validate the labels; it asks
how many adversarial label changes would be required to overturn the result.
